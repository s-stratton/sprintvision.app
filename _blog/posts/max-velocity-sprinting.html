Once you’re upright, the job changes. You’re no longer building speed through long pushes; you’re applying as much force as possible in a ground contact that lasts under a tenth of a second in elite sprinters. Each step takes about a fifth of a second in total. The positions you hit in that time decide how much of your force moves you forward and how much is lost to braking.

Most of the numbers here come from a 2019 study in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living that measured sprinters at full speed, averaging 9.9 m/s, and looked at how their mechanics changed with running speed, leg length and step characteristics.

Posture

At top speed the trunk should be close to vertical: 0–5° of forward lean for senior sprinters, 5–10° for sub-elite. The hips stay high and the pelvis level. Sitting back, with the hips behind you and a slight bend at the waist, moves the foot strike further in front of the body and shortens the swing. Leaning back does the same.

When you check posture on video, hold the phone level. A tilted phone can add a few degrees in either direction.

Touchdown: where speed is lost

Strike under your centre of mass

The foot should land close to underneath the hips, with the shin near vertical at touchdown; in fast sprinters it’s about 5° past vertical. A foot that lands out in front of the knee, with the shin angled back, is overstriding. That step lands ahead of your centre of mass, so the first part of every contact is a braking force you then have to push through. It’s one of the most common faults in developing sprinters.

Cue: “Strike down and back, under the hips.” Drill: wicket runs, with mini-hurdles spaced to your step length.

Dorsiflexed ankle, stiff knee

Fast sprinters hold the ankle dorsiflexed, toes pulled up towards the shin, as the foot comes down, so they land on the ball of the foot with the ankle already stiff. The knee is only slightly bent at touchdown, about 152°, and gives very little through contact. Together that makes the leg behave like a stiff spring: it takes several times body weight and returns it quickly instead of absorbing it.

A knee much more bent at touchdown, under about 145°, collapses on contact and you sink. A knee close to straight, over about 160°, usually means the foot is reaching forward.

Cue: “Stiff, active leg.” Drill: pogo jumps and straight-leg bounds.

Front-side mechanics

Thigh drive

At its highest, the swing thigh reaches about 70° from vertical in sprinters at 9–11 m/s, just below horizontal. Coaches call this front-side mechanics: most of the leg action happens in front of the body, so the foot is high and ready to strike down. A thigh that only reaches 55–60° usually goes with a long, low swing and a foot strike out in front.

Heel recovery

As the foot leaves the ground, the heel folds up under the hips. At top speed, sprinters close the knee to about 32° at its tightest. A tighter fold shortens the swing leg, so it recovers faster. In novice sprinters it ranges from about 32° to 49°.

Keep it in proportion

Front-side mechanics are among the most coached features of sprinting, but research by Haugen and colleagues (2018) found that once sprinters were at speed, neither front-side nor back-side measures clearly separated faster from slower trained sprinters. Use thigh drive and heel recovery to spot clear problems, not as the main thing to chase. For a structured video screen built around hamstring injury risk, see the Sprint Mechanics Assessment Score.

Arm action

At top speed the elbows stay near 90°, opening slightly on the back swing, with the drive coming from the shoulder. Faster sprinters move the upper arm through a bigger range: in Ralph Mann’s analysis of 200 m finalists, about 135° against about 118° for slower sprinters. Long, straight arms swing slowly; arms held too tightly bent swing short.

Step rate

Sprinters at 9–11 m/s take about 4.6 steps per second. But the classic study on what limits top speed (Weyand and colleagues, 2000) found that faster runners don’t reposition their legs much faster through the air. They apply more force to the ground in a shorter contact. Turnover comes from force, not from trying to move your legs quickly. More on this in how to increase stride length and frequency.

Max velocity drills

Each drill isolates one part of the top-speed action, so you can rehearse it at a speed where you can control it.

DrillWhat it trainsKey cue
A-march and A-skipThigh drive and front-side mechanics“Step over the opposite knee”
DribblesHeel recovery and striking under the hips“Heel to hip, foot down under you”
Straight-leg boundsA stiff leg at touchdown“Paw the ground back”
Pogo jumpsAnkle stiffness and short contacts“Toes up, minimal ground time”
Wicket runsRhythm, posture and cleaning up overstriding“Run tall, strike down”

For wicket runs, place mini-hurdles or flat markers in a line, spaced roughly to your step length at speed, and run over them at close to full speed. The spacing forces the foot to strike under your body.

Drills teach the positions, but only sprinting at full speed makes them automatic. What matters is whether the foot still strikes under your hips in a flying run, not just in drills.

How to check your top-speed mechanics

Film a flying run side-on, or follow the athlete with the camera, with the whole body in shot and the phone held level. Normal-speed video is enough for these angles. See the filming guide.
